You've heard the buzz — literally. That sound is part of the tattoo experience,
and it tells you something about the tool in the artist's hand. There are three
families of machine in use today, and while the **machine never makes the
artist**, it does shape how a line goes down. Here's the honest tour.

## Coil machines — the classic buzz

The iconic tattoo machine: an electromagnetic coil snaps the needle up and down,
and that's where the unmistakable buzz comes from. Coils hit hard and precise,
which is why a lot of traditional artists swear by them — especially for crisp,
single-pass [lining](/blog/tattoo-techniques-single-needle-whip-shading-stippling/).
They're heavier, they need tuning, and running one well is a craft in itself.
Liners and shaders are usually separate machines, so you'll see a coil artist
switch tools mid-session.

## Rotary machines — smooth and quiet

A small motor drives the needle in a smooth, continuous motion instead of a snap.
Rotaries are lighter, quieter, gentler on the skin, and versatile enough to line
*and* shade with one machine. That do-everything flexibility has made them the
modern default for a huge share of artists, especially for smooth color packing
and soft black-and-grey.

## Wireless pens — the modern workhorse

The newest evolution: a rotary motor built into a pen-shaped body with the battery
on board. No cords, no clip cord dragging across your arm, total freedom of
movement around the body. They've exploded in popularity for exactly that reason.
A good wireless pen is a convenience and ergonomics upgrade — not a shortcut, and
not a substitute for skill.

## What this means for you as a client

Don't pick an artist by their machine. Pick them by their
[healed work](/blog/how-we-crown-the-best-tattoo-artist/). A master with a
forty-year-old coil will out-tattoo a beginner with the priciest wireless pen on
the market, every time. The machine is just the brush — what matters is the hand,
and a [clean station](/blog/whats-in-the-tray-tattoo-station-tour/) around it.
