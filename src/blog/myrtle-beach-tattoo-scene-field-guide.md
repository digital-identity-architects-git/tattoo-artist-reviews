Most people drive into Myrtle Beach for the SkyWheel, the boardwalk, and the
mini-golf. They drive out with a tattoo. That's been the rhythm of this town for
decades — and underneath the neon and the souvenir shops, the Grand Strand has
quietly grown into one of the densest tattoo markets on the East Coast.

This is the first post in our Myrtle Beach series. We're studying the scene
city-wide before we hand anyone the crown — and we want the artists who live here
to put themselves in the running. More on that at the bottom.

## A real tattoo town, not a tourist gimmick

Myrtle Beach didn't get its reputation by accident. South Carolina was one of the
last states in the country to legalize tattooing — the ban didn't lift until
2004 — which means the shops that survived the gray-market years and went legit
first carry genuine history. Myrtle Beach Ink, run by multi-award-winning artist
Eddie Kane, is recognized as the first legalized tattoo studio in the state. When
a scene is *that* hard-won, the people in it tend to take the craft seriously.

## The lay of the land

The Grand Strand stretches roughly sixty miles, and the ink is spread across all
of it — from North Myrtle Beach down through the boardwalk district and out to the
shops clustered near Coastal Grand Mall. If you want the single highest
concentration of talent, though, you go to **Seaboard Street** — a short
industrial stretch off Highway 17 that's become the city's unofficial tattoo row.
We gave it [its own field guide](/blog/seaboard-street-myrtle-beach-tattoo-row/).

The styles run the full range: bold American traditional built for the beach
crowd, Japanese, black-and-grey realism, fine-line, and cover-ups (a vacation-town
specialty — somebody's always fixing a mistake from last summer). The local "Best
of the Beach" awards have become a real signal here; shops chase them year after
year, and that competition keeps standards high.
